Zoning Bylaw Rule Verifier — M7

Speech note · what to say in the meeting · ~7–8 minutes with the code walkthrough

“Propose with AI. Decide with deterministic rules. Let nothing else be the authority.”

1 Opening — what we built

Every Canadian city publishes its zoning bylaw as a long legal PDF. Calgary’s runs to over a thousand pages. Buried inside that text are the rules planners and GIS teams need every day — things like “the minimum side setback is one-point-two metres.” The problem is that those rules are written for lawyers, not for software, and they hide in two very different places: in ordinary prose, and in dense dimensional tables. What we built reads the whole bylaw, asks an AI to propose candidate rules, and then runs every candidate through a deterministic verifier that only accepts a rule if its exact value, unit, direction, and scope are literally there in the cited source text. The one idea to walk away with: the AI is allowed to suggest, but only the verifier is allowed to decide.

2 Why this is hard

It would be easy to assume you could just hand the PDF to a large language model and ask for the rules — but that is the trap. Independent studies this past year show legal AI tools still hallucinate between seventeen and thirty-three percent of their citations, even when handed the source text. On top of that, the same number can mean completely different things depending on context — “one-point-two metres” might be a side setback, an easement width, or the height of a fence — and if you get that association wrong, you have just published a rule the bylaw never stated. The cost of being wrong is real: a bad setback could mis-approve a building. So the system’s first duty is a negative one — never verify something the bylaw does not say, even if that means leaving some genuinely true rules in a human-review queue.

3 The architecture — one trust boundary

The whole design is organised around a single trust boundary, and this diagram is the heart of the talk. On the left is the proposer tier, and everything there is allowed to be wrong. On the right, inside the thick green border, is the authority tier — plain, auditable code with no AI in it at all. The line for the slide: extraction proposes, verification proves, and the GIS layer consumes only verified rules. Let me now walk through each box and show you the single most important piece of code behind it.

4 Inside each part — with the code

PART 1 · Page-scope the PDF extraction/pdf_ingest.py · native_extraction.py

Before we read a single rule, we cut the thousand-page document down to just the pages that govern the zone we care about. This matters for honesty as much as speed: if we extracted from the whole book, our “recall” would be measured against a denominator that includes hundreds of irrelevant pages. So the very first thing the pipeline does is restrict the document to the pages declared in provenance.

PART 2 · Retrieval & the rule-signal sweep native_extraction.py

Within those pages we don’t just dump everything at the model. We build targeted queries from the zone’s own vocabulary — setback, height, lot coverage, and so on — and retrieve the passages most likely to contain a rule, then sweep for rule-like signals (a regulated object next to a number). This is the “find the needle” step; its only job is to surface candidate text, never to judge it.

Point to: the “Retrieval / RAG” box.

PART 3 · The matrix parser — tables, cell by cell table_natural_logic.py

This is the part I’m proudest of, and it’s the big M7 unlock. Most rules in modern bylaws live in dimensional tables — a grid of dwelling-type against value. Instead of asking the model to “read the table,” we read it deterministically: each cell becomes one candidate, and we prove it from the table’s own geometry. The title tells us the rule family, the row and column headers tell us the scope, and the cell tells us the value. No language model is in this loop, so it cannot hallucinate a cell that isn’t there.

Point to: the “Matrix parser (deterministic)” box.

# table_natural_logic.py — prove a table cell WITHOUT an LLM
def table_proof_trace(candidate, evidence, ...):
    row_header    = str(evidence.get("row_header") or "")
    column_header = str(evidence.get("column_header") or "")
    cell_value    = str(evidence.get("cell_value") or "")
    return {
        # title proves the family: "Minimum Lot Line Setbacks"
        "rule_object": _prove_rule_object(candidate, table_title, row_header, ...),
        # row/column prove the scope: "Lane Yard", "Rear Principal"
        "scope":       _prove_text_claim(candidate.get("constraint_scope"), ...),
        # operator comes from words like Minimum/Maximum, not the cell
    }
PART 4 · The LLM extractor — a leashed proposer native_extraction.py

For the prose rules we do use the language model — but notice how tightly it’s leashed by its own prompt. We tell it to extract only what is visibly in this exact text, never to infer a missing value, never to use outside knowledge, and — crucially — if it can’t see whether a rule is a minimum or a maximum, to leave the operator blank rather than guess. A blank operator simply sends the candidate to review. The model is rewarded for honesty, not for filling in blanks.

Point to: the “LLM extraction · gemini-3.1” box.

# native_extraction.py — the proposer is constrained by its prompt

"Do not infer missing values. Do not use outside knowledge."
"If the operator/direction wording is not visible, set operator to \"\""
"  instead of guessing or dropping the rule;"
"  incomplete candidates go to review downstream."
PART 5 · The one internal contract rule_candidates + evidence_units

Every path — the matrix parser, the LLM, and even legacy upstream extractors — funnels into one identical shape. A candidate is just structured fields plus a pointer to the evidence that supposedly supports it. Standardising on this contract is what lets a single verifier judge everything the same way, no matter where the candidate came from.

PART 7 · The gates — the direction check support_checks.py

Here is one of those gates, and it’s the one that catches the most dangerous error: a flipped direction. If the model claims a value is a maximum, the evidence text has to actually contain maximum-type wording — “maximum,” “not exceed,” “up to.” If it claims a minimum, it needs “minimum,” “not less,” “at least.” A number with no matching direction word in the source cannot pass. That single check is what stops a minimum setback from being published as a maximum, which is the textbook way a false rule slips through.

Point to: “support_checks.py — value · unit · direction · scope”.

def operator_supported(candidate, evidence_text) -> bool:
    operator = {"≤":"<=", "≥":">="}.get(operator, operator)  # canonicalize
    text = evidence_text.lower()
    if operator in {"<=","max","maximum"}:
        return any(p in text for p in ["maximum","not exceed","up to","limited to"])
    if operator in {">=","min","minimum"}:
        return any(p in text for p in ["minimum","not less","no less","at least"])

PART 8 · Gaps → bucket — the decision decision_policy.py

And this is the climax — the entire safety story collapses into one short function anyone in this room could read. The first line is the whole philosophy: a rule is verified only when there are zero blocking gaps — only when every gate passed. There is no confidence score, no “the model seemed pretty sure.” Proven or not. And notice the order: critical rejections first, then not-used, then review. When the system is in any doubt, it falls toward safety, never toward verified. Because this is deterministic, the same candidate with the same evidence gives the same bucket every single time — which is exactly what lets us test it and audit it by hand.

Point to: “decision_policy.py — gaps → bucket”, then the four bucket chips.

# src/burnaby_prototype/decision_policy.py
def verification_decision_from_gaps(support_gaps) -> str:
    gaps = set(support_gaps)
    blocking = gaps - ADVISORY_GAPS
    if not blocking:                          # every gate passed
        return VERIFIED
    if CRITICAL_REJECTION_GAPS & blocking:    # poisoned / contradicted
        return REJECTED
    if NOT_USED_GAPS & blocking:              # real artifact, not a rule
        return NOT_USED
    return REVIEW_NEEDED                       # plausible, needs a human

5 A concrete example — the bug it caught

Let me make that tangible. Suppose the model proposes “side setback, minimum one-point-two metres.” Before that can ever be trusted, the verifier asks a series of literal questions through the gates you just saw. Is one-point-two actually in the quoted clause? Is the unit metres? Is it a minimum and not a maximum? Is the clause genuinely about a setback? Is it the right scope — the side line, this zone? In Calgary, this is exactly what saved us. The extractor had proposed a one-point-two metre side setback, but when the verifier checked the rule object and the surrounding clause, the real source turned out to be a one-point-two metre easement width — the same number, attached to a completely different rule. The verifier blocked it. That single catch is why our Calgary count is twenty-nine verified rules and not thirty.

6 The results, and an honest caveat

Across all three pilot cities, audited against the real PDFs: Burnaby 87 verified, Vancouver 10, Calgary 29 — verified precision 1.00 everywhere, and zero false-verifies. Four hundred and eighty-five tests pass, and the adversarial poison cases are all blocked.

What changed in M7 is that we taught the system to read the dimensional tables cell by cell instead of only the prose; we fed Calgary the full thousand-page bylaw scoped to the right pages rather than a small slice; and we settled on a single strongest model instead of a noisy bake-off. Calgary alone went from sixteen verified rules to twenty-nine. But here is the honest part, and it’s the most important sentence in the whole talk: every one of those recall gains came from better extraction, not from relaxing the verifier. We actually tried a change on the verifier side to lift the numbers further, and it started letting false rules through — so we reverted it. The verifier sits at its safe ceiling, and we chose to keep it there.

7 Closing — why you can trust it

So why can you trust this? Because the power is deliberately separated: the creative AI literally cannot promote a rule — only the deterministic code can, and a test enforces that the verification path never even imports the AI modules. Because we attack it on every build with poisoned candidates that have to be blocked. Because we audited the verified rules against the actual bylaws, not just our own answer key — which is how the Calgary easement bug surfaced. And because the answer key itself is never visible to the runtime, so it cannot leak. The hallway version, if someone stops you afterward: the AI suggests, the deterministic verifier decides, and it has never once verified a rule the bylaw doesn’t say.
